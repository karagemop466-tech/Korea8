Scope & access

From above or from below: what each route requires, what it costs in disruption, and what must be decided before anyone cuts.

This page describes a decision process for a licensed contractor to execute. It is not a do-it-yourself instruction and it is not a diagnosis. Nothing here is based on having seen the tub, the wall, or the plumbing.

Option A — Extract the linkage from the overflow opening (from above)

What happens: Remove the two screws holding the overflow plate, pull the plate and the attached linkage/plunger straight out of the overflow tube, free or replace the plunger barrel, reinstall with a new overflow gasket.

Evidence for: Manufacturer documentation for concealed bath drains shows the overflow side as the service path for the control linkage (S42). Standard trip-lever bath waste construction puts the plunger barrel in the overflow tube above the drain tee.

Evidence against: None official. The user reports that five or more licensed plumbers declined this route, which is user-reported only (U-01).

Opens drywall: No · Tenant impact: Lowest: no dust, no ceiling access, typically one visit.

If it fails: The plunger barrel or linkage may remain stuck inside the overflow tube. Fishing it out with a hooked wire or a claw tool is the next step; if that fails, option B.

Unverified prerequisites:

  • The overflow plate screws are not seized or stripped
  • The linkage is still attached to the plate (not snapped off inside the tube)
  • There is enough clearance in the overflow tube for the plunger to slide up

Label: MIXED - manufacturer documentation verified; the specific seized condition is user-reported · Sources: S42 S33

Option B — Open the ceiling of the storage unit below (from below)

What happens: Cut an access opening in the storage-unit ceiling, reach the waste-and-overflow tee and the trap, release the slip nut or cut and replace the assembly, then patch and paint the ceiling.

Evidence for: SF.gov states a permit is needed before cutting into or replacing pipes, particularly pipes covered by a wall (S20); that is only a statement of when a permit is triggered, not that this route is required. The trap tee sits below the overflow, behind the tub apron, so ceiling access reaches the slip joint that overflow-side work cannot (S33).

Evidence against: Requires a permit under the ordinary reading of S20/S21 plus a drywall trade, dust control, and a second space to access. It is the most disruptive option.

Opens drywall: Yes · Tenant impact: Highest: dust, noise, a hole in a tenant's ceiling, two visits minimum (open, then close after inspection).

If it fails: It generally does not fail; ceiling access is the deterministic route. The risk moves to damage and cost rather than feasibility.

Unverified prerequisites:

  • The plumbing run is actually above the storage unit's ceiling in an accessible location
  • Clearance and safe working height under the ceiling
  • No asbestos-containing ceiling material, no concealed wiring or ductwork in the cut zone
  • The tenant in the storage unit consents to access and the work

Label: MIXED - permit trigger verified; feasibility and timing are inference · Sources: S20 S21 S33 S34

Option C — Install or restore a 12-inch access panel and service through it

What happens: Instead of a plain drywall patch, close the opening with a removable access panel of at least 12 in. in its least dimension so the next failure is serviceable without demolition.

Evidence for: Code requires a 12 in. access panel or utility space where slip-joint connections are concealed (S33). This makes the current defect and future service the same problem, solved once.

Evidence against: Cosmetic impact in a rented storage unit; the owner may prefer a flush patch.

Opens drywall: Yes · Tenant impact: Same as option B on the day, better on every future visit.

If it fails: Not applicable - an access panel is a permanent improvement.

Unverified prerequisites:

  • A suitable wall or ceiling location adjacent to the tee that satisfies the 12 in. minimum

Label: OFFICIAL for the code requirement; this is a design recommendation · Sources: S33 S44

Why plumbers may have declined

HYPOTHESIS - not verified These are plausible industry reasons assembled from the code text and manufacturer documentation. They are NOT statements from any of the plumbers who declined, and no plumber's reason is recorded in this repository.
  • A seized plunger barrel in a corroded overflow tube can shear the linkage, and a sheared linkage inside a concealed pipe converts a small repair into a demolition job with no clean stopping point.
  • The overflow tube may be a soldered or glued assembly rather than a slip-joint assembly, so nothing inside it is designed to come apart from the tub side.
  • A contractor who cannot see the tee cannot warrant the result, and a $135-$614 price cannot absorb an unknown.
  • Liability in an occupied building: a failed attempt that damages the ceiling below becomes the plumber's problem.
